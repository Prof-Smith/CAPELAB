A note to students: valuation, caution, and the CAPE ratio

I have been thinking a lot about valuations recently. Most of the time I avoid making directional market predictions because those forecasts are occasionally right, but often unreliable. The value investor in me still favors proceeding with caution, preserving a margin of safety, and avoiding the temptation to confuse expensive markets with precisely timed crash predictions.

One valuation measure worth studying is the CAPE ratio, or cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio. CAPE compares the current level of the market with inflation-adjusted earnings averaged over the previous ten years. The purpose of the ten-year earnings average is to smooth out unusually strong or unusually weak earnings years and give students a longer-term view of valuation.

The key lesson is not simply that a high CAPE means “sell” or that a low CAPE means “buy.” A high CAPE may suggest that future long-run returns could be lower than usual, but it does not tell us exactly when a downturn will occur. Markets can remain expensive for extended periods. CAPE is therefore better understood as a tool for thinking about valuation risk and expected returns, not as a precise market-timing signal.

This lab asks you to test that idea visually. Use the charts below to compare high-CAPE periods with later drawdowns and forward returns. The goal is to distinguish between three ideas: high valuation, future return expectations, and the timing of market declines.

Core lesson

CAPE is not a crash predictor. CAPE is a valuation tool that helps investors think about long-run expected returns, valuation risk, and the discipline required when prices are high relative to smoothed earnings.

How to read this dashboard

  1. Start with the current CAPE level and percentile. This tells you how unusual today’s valuation is relative to market history.
  2. Use the CAPE threshold slider. Highlight past periods when valuations were similarly elevated.
  3. Compare valuation with drawdowns. Ask whether high valuation reliably identified the start of a downturn.
  4. Review forward returns. Test whether CAPE appears more useful for long-run expected returns than short-run timing.
  5. Make a decision. Decide how a disciplined investor should respond when valuations are historically expensive.

Historical analog caution

Historical analog estimates are not forecasts. They summarize what happened after past periods with similar CAPE levels. Treat them as base-rate evidence, not as a prediction of what must happen next.

What CAPE measures

CAPE compares market price to the average of the previous ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings.

What CAPE can help with

CAPE can help frame long-run expected return risk when prices are high relative to smoothed earnings.

What CAPE cannot do

CAPE does not reliably identify the exact month or year when a market downturn will begin.

Plain-English valuation guide

CAPE RangeInterpretationDecision question
Below 15Historically inexpensiveIs the market cheap because future fundamentals are impaired, or because investors are too pessimistic?
15 to 25Normal to moderately elevatedAre expected returns reasonable for the risk being taken?
25 to 35ExpensiveWhat margin of safety remains if earnings disappoint?
Above 35Historically extremeShould a disciplined investor reduce risk, rebalance, or simply lower future return expectations?

Big question

If valuation is poor at timing crashes but useful for estimating long-run return pressure, how should a disciplined investor respond to historically expensive markets?

Student reflection questions

  1. Does a high CAPE ratio reliably predict an imminent market crash?
  2. Does CAPE appear more useful for estimating long-run future returns than short-run market timing?
  3. If CAPE is near a historical extreme, should an investor sell, reduce risk, continue dollar-cost averaging, or ignore valuation?
  4. How would your answer change if your investment horizon were one year, ten years, or thirty years?
  5. What does CAPE help you understand about expected returns, and what does it fail to tell you about timing?
